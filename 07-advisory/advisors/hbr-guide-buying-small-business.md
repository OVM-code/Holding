## What this book is and why it's on the roster

A step-by-step playbook for a first-time buyer acquiring a single small,
"enduringly profitable" business — from deciding whether acquisition
entrepreneurship suits you at all, through funding and running a search,
sourcing, filtering, diligence, valuation, financing, and closing, to the
first days as owner. It is on the roster because this holding's principal
has no prior acquisition experience, and this is the canonical
first-timer's manual for exactly the phase the holding is in: the search
for and purchase of deal #1.

Structure (verified): five parts, 21 chapters, a conclusion, and two
appendices reproducing a real Indication of Intent and Letter of Intent
(for Zeswitz Music). Part One — Think Big, Buy Small (ch. 1–3); Part Two —
Preparing for Your Search (ch. 4–6); Part Three — Finding the Right Small
Business to Buy (ch. 7–12); Part Four — Making an Offer (ch. 13–16); Part
Five — Completing the Acquisition (ch. 17–21).

## Core frameworks (as the book states them)

- **Entrepreneurship through acquisition (ETA)** — you don't need a
  breakthrough idea to be an entrepreneur; buying an existing profitable
  business skips the startup death-valley and puts you straight into
  running something that already works. The honest self-assessment
  (ch. 2): ownership suits people who want to manage and lead, tolerate
  unglamorous industries, and accept the financial and personal
  commitment of a multi-year search; it does not suit people who need
  prestige industries or hate sales. (Part One, ch. 1–2)
- **The search is a job, not a hobby** — a full-time search takes roughly
  six months to two years, plus no less than about three more months from
  accepted offer to close; budget for your living costs and search
  expenses up front, and decide deliberately between self-funding the
  search and raising a search fund (investors fund the search in exchange
  for the right to invest in the deal). (Part Two, ch. 4–5)
- **Enduring profitability = recurring customers** — the essential
  characteristic of the business worth buying is an established base of
  recurring customers/revenue (maintenance contracts, repeat purchases,
  high switching costs, niche dominance), which makes profits persist
  through a change of owner. Slow-growing, "boring," recurring-revenue
  businesses are the target; fast-growth and high-tech businesses are
  explicitly *not*, because growth consumes cash and competes away
  margins. The book's sweet spot: EBITDA between roughly $750,000 and
  $2 million. (Part Three, ch. 10; financial confirmation via historical
  statements in ch. 11)
- **The dual filter** — a prospect is only worth real time if it passes
  *both* tests: (a) the business is enduringly profitable, and (b) the
  owner is genuinely committed to selling. Ambivalent sellers are the
  great time-sink of a search; test commitment early (why are they
  selling, what will they do after, what price do they expect) and drop
  prospects whose owners are window-shopping. (Part Three, ch. 10 and 12)
- **Two sourcing channels, run in parallel** — brokered deals (higher
  volume, pre-committed sellers, but competition and broker-groomed
  numbers) and direct/proprietary sourcing (letters and calls to owners
  in chosen niches: no competition, but many ambivalent sellers and a
  long funnel). Manage the search like a pipeline with fast, cheap
  filters so that most prospects are killed in minutes, not weeks.
  (Part Three, ch. 7–9)
- **Preliminary vs. confirmatory due diligence** — spend your own time
  first and other people's fees later. Preliminary diligence (days, before
  the offer) hunts deal-killers and builds your own model of owner
  earnings from the numbers provided; confirmatory diligence (after a
  signed LOI with exclusivity) is the expensive, exhaustive phase with
  accountants (quality-of-earnings report) and lawyers, verifying
  everything you relied on. (Part Four ch. 13; Part Five ch. 17)
- **Small-business valuation** — enduringly profitable small firms in the
  book's size range trade at roughly 3–5× EBITDA. They are cheap relative
  to large companies for structural reasons (illiquidity, key-person and
  owner-dependence risk, thin buyer pool), and that discount is the
  buyer's source of return — not clever turnaround plans. Price off
  demonstrated, normalized past earnings, never off seller projections.
  (Part Four, ch. 14)
- **Financing stack** — an illustrative structure of roughly 40% senior
  bank debt, 20–25% seller note, ~35% equity. The seller note (typically
  4–5 year term, ~5–8% fixed) matters beyond the money: a seller who
  refuses to hold paper is telling you something about the business.
  Earn-outs defer price contingent on performance. Outside equity for
  deals this size is findable but expensive — investors expect on the
  order of 25% annual returns for the risk and illiquidity. (Part Four
  ch. 15; Part Five ch. 18–19)
- **Offer mechanics** — an IOI to signal serious interest and price
  range early, then a non-binding LOI that fixes price, structure, and an
  exclusivity period before you spend heavily on confirmatory diligence.
  Real examples in Appendices A and B. (Part Four ch. 16; Appendices)

## The book's method, step by step

This is the procedure the seat runs. It is a *sequential gate* process —
each stage exists to kill prospects cheaply before the next, more
expensive stage.

1. **Decide whether to do this at all** (ch. 1–3). Assess honestly:
   appetite for managing people, tolerance for unglamorous work, family
   and financial runway for a 1–2 year search, willingness to
   personally guarantee debt. If the answer is no, stop here.
2. **Fund and scope the search** (ch. 4–6). Budget the full-time search
   (living costs + search expenses over up to two years); choose
   self-funded vs. sponsored search; write down the target profile
   *before* looking — size band, recurring-revenue character, geography,
   industries you'll accept — so every prospect can be filtered against
   it mechanically.
3. **Build the pipeline** (ch. 7–9). Work brokers systematically (become
   a known, credible, responsive buyer so they send you deals early) and
   run a parallel direct-outreach program into chosen niches. Track
   everything; expect to review hundreds of prospects per closed deal;
   filter in minutes using the target profile.
4. **Apply the dual filter** (ch. 10–12). Enduring profitability first:
   recurring customers, stable margins over multiple years of historical
   financials, low customer concentration, profits that survive the
   owner's exit. Then owner commitment: reason for selling, price
   expectation stated early, life-after-sale plan. Fail either → drop
   without regret.
5. **Preliminary due diligence** (ch. 13). A focused burst of your own
   time: rebuild the earnings from the raw numbers (normalize the
   owner's salary and personal expenses), identify the handful of
   assumptions the deal depends on, look for deal-killers (customer
   concentration, owner-held relationships, deferred capex, litigation).
   Only proceed if you'd be happy owning it at a price you can now name.
6. **Price and structure the offer** (ch. 14–16). Value at 3–5× normalized
   EBITDA depending on quality (recurrence, growth, concentration);
   sketch the financing stack (senior debt / seller note / equity) so the
   offer is fundable; send an IOI, negotiate to a signed LOI with
   exclusivity.
7. **Confirmatory due diligence and money-raising in parallel** (ch.
   17–19). Engage accountants for a quality-of-earnings review and
   lawyers for legal diligence; simultaneously raise the senior debt and
   the equity, since financing contingencies and diligence findings feed
   each other. This is the most time-intensive phase — expect ~3+ months.
8. **Negotiate the purchase agreement and close** (ch. 20–21). Convert
   the LOI into the binding agreement — reps and warranties, indemnities,
   escrows, the asset-vs-stock structure (US framing) — then closing-day
   mechanics and funds flow.
9. **The first days as owner** (ch. 21 and Conclusion). Communicate
   continuity to employees and customers, learn the business from the
   inside before changing anything significant, and manage the cash
   weekly. The value was bought at the price paid; the first year's job
   is not to break what makes the profits recur.

## Where the book applies to this holding — and where it doesn't

**Written for:** a US individual searcher (typically an MBA) buying *one*
company of ~$750k–$2M EBITDA at 3–5× EBITDA, financed with US bank/SBA
debt, seller notes, and search-fund-style equity, in the mid-2010s. The
legal machinery (asset vs. stock purchase tax treatment, SBA loans, LOI
custom, quality-of-earnings practice) is US practice.

**This holding:** a Belgian holding company buying *multiple* SMEs to
hold, targeting sound-but-underperforming businesses, with a
service-first → option-to-buy structure. Differences that mark where
`[extrapolation]` starts:

- **Geography/law.** Everything the book says about deal structure,
  taxes, SBA/bank debt, and closing mechanics is US-specific. Belgian
  share-vs-asset deal treatment, notarial practice, and financing markets
  differ; the *logic* (buyer prefers not to inherit hidden liabilities;
  seller paper aligns incentives) travels, the mechanics do not.
  [extrapolation — and per repo convention, Belgian counsel required.]
- **Underperformance vs. enduring profitability.** The book explicitly
  wants *already-well-performing* dull businesses and warns against
  turnarounds and fixer-uppers. This holding's thesis (sound but
  underperforming, operator-gap or cost-structure verdicts) is adjacent
  but not identical: the book's seat will push hard on whether the
  recurring-customer base is intact — its test is compatible with an
  operator-gap thesis but hostile to anything shading toward demand
  decay. Applying its filters to deliberately underperforming targets is
  [extrapolation], and productive friction with the house thesis.
- **Size band.** Belgian SME targets may sit below the book's
  $750k–$2M EBITDA band; the book itself warns that smaller businesses
  are riskier (thinner management, more owner-dependent) — below-band
  application is [extrapolation].
- **One deal vs. a portfolio; buyer becomes CEO vs. holding + hired
  operators; the option-to-buy structure.** All outside the book's text.
  The search/filter/diligence/valuation machinery still applies per deal.
  [extrapolation]

## Research workflow for a board question

1. Restate the question in the book's vocabulary: which *stage gate* is
   this — search design, sourcing, dual filter, preliminary diligence,
   offer/valuation, confirmatory diligence/financing, or first-year
   ownership?
2. Locate the governing chapter(s) from the part/chapter map above.
3. Run the book's procedure on the specifics: e.g. for a candidate in
   `01-deals/`, apply the dual filter (recurring customers + owner
   commitment) before any valuation talk; for a price question, normalize
   EBITDA first, then place it in the 3–5× band by quality; for a
   structure question, start from the 40/25/35-style stack and ask
   whether the seller will hold paper.
4. State plainly where the question leaves the book's scope — Belgian
   law and tax, sub-scale targets, turnaround theses, portfolio
   construction, the option-to-buy structure — flag it `[extrapolation]`
   or hand it to another seat, rather than improvising in the book's
   voice.

## What the book would say "no" to

- **Startups and "great ideas"** — the whole premise is buying proven
  profits, not creating them. (Ch. 1)
- **Fast-growing or high-tech businesses** — growth consumes cash,
  attracts competition, and makes the future unlike the past you paid
  for; the book wants dull and steady. (Ch. 10)
- **Businesses without recurring customers** — project-based,
  hit-driven, or one-off-sale revenue fails its central test of enduring
  profitability. (Ch. 10)
- **Turnarounds and broken businesses** — it explicitly steers first-time
  buyers away from businesses that need fixing to be worth owning. This
  is the seat's standing challenge to every "underperforming" candidate
  in this pipeline: prove the profits are enduring *now*, with the
  underperformance located in the owner, not the demand. (Ch. 10–11)
- **Ambivalent sellers** — an owner who won't name a price expectation
  or has no life-after-sale plan is not a seller yet; walk away early.
  (Ch. 12)
- **Paying for projections** — price is set on demonstrated, normalized
  historical earnings; a multiple above the 3–5× band needs the quality
  to justify it, not a growth story. (Ch. 11, 14)
- **A seller who won't hold a note** — refusal to keep skin in the game
  is a diligence red flag in itself. (Ch. 15)
- **Skipping the search discipline** — falling in love with an early
  prospect, skipping preliminary diligence, or spending confirmatory-
  diligence money before a signed LOI with exclusivity. (Ch. 7, 13, 16)
- **Buying part-time on the side** — the book treats a serious search as
  a full-time, funded commitment; a casual search yields casual results.
  (Ch. 4) [Its half-hearted-search warning applies to this holding's
  principal in proportion to time actually committed — application is
  [extrapolation].]
