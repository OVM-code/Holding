## What this book is and why it's on the roster

A practitioner's manual arguing that buying an existing profitable small
business beats starting one — you skip the startup "death valley" and
begin with revenue, customers, and infrastructure — and then walking the
first-time buyer through self-assessment, target definition, search,
valuation, deal execution, and the first ninety days. It's on the roster
because this holding's owner is a first-time acquirer doing exactly what
the book teaches: buying sound-but-underperforming SMEs from exit-seeking
owners, and it should be heard on target profiling, search/funnel
discipline, buyer-fit questions, offer/valuation posture, and post-close
transition.

## Core frameworks (as the book states them)

Book structure: Part 1 *Opportunity* (Ch. 1–2), Part 2 *Evaluation*
(Ch. 3–5), Part 3 *Analysis* (Ch. 6–9), Part 4 *Execution* (Ch. 10–12),
plus a conclusion ("Acquisition in the Entrepreneurship Economy").

- **Buy then build (acquisition entrepreneurship)** — most startups fail
  before ever reaching product-market fit; acquiring an existing company
  means starting with profitable infrastructure — customers, employees,
  cash flow — and applying entrepreneurial energy to *growth* instead of
  *survival*. (Ch. 1, "Don't Start a Business")
- **Engineering wealth through leverage** — a small acquisition financed
  largely with debt (in the book's US context, an SBA 7(a) loan at ~90%
  leverage) lets the business's own existing cash flow service the debt
  and produce outsized returns on the buyer's equity. (Ch. 2,
  "Engineering Wealth") — the leverage *principle* travels; the SBA
  mechanics do not (see scope section).
- **The CEO mindset and the three A's — Attitude, Aptitude, Action** —
  before defining a target, assess yourself. *Attitude*: a growth
  mindset — relentless improvement, learning from mistakes, comfort
  managing risk — as the top predictor of success. *Aptitude*: your
  actual skills and competencies; the book stresses that creativity and
  problem-solving-as-issues-arise matter more than precision and rigid
  organization. *Action*: your propensity to execute — what you will
  actually do as an owner-operator. The right target is where all three
  align with the opportunity. (Ch. 3, "The CEO Mindset")
- **The opportunity-profile matrix (the "AE matrix")** — four acquisition
  profiles along growth and value dimensions, each demanding a different
  CEO: **Eternally Profitable** (stable cash-cow, low growth, low
  disruption risk — bought to reduce risk), **Turnaround** (a company
  fallen on hard times — value created by fixing operations and
  rebuilding customer value; highest execution demand), **High Growth**
  (fast-growing, capital-hungry — the job is managing growth), and
  **Platform** (a sound company offering both value and growth potential,
  which the book calls the sweet spot for an owner-operator CEO).
  (Ch. 4, "Defining the Target")
- **The target statement** — a one-sentence search filter, in the book's
  template: *"I am looking for a [product, distribution, or service]
  company with [type of growth opportunity], generating [SDE range],
  with [limiters]."* Everything in the search is scored against it;
  deals that don't fit are passed on quickly regardless of how shiny
  they look. (Ch. 4)
- **Search as a disciplined funnel, not browsing** — treat the search
  like a full-time job with a defined pipeline: broker relationships,
  fast and professional responses to listings/CIMs, rapid triage against
  the target statement, and acceptance that the overwhelming majority of
  reviewed opportunities die before LOI. Being a credible, prepared,
  decisive buyer is itself a source of deal flow. (Ch. 5, "The Search";
  Ch. 6, "Deal Making")
- **"Buy for the future, pay for the past"** — value the business on its
  *historical* proven earnings (SDE/adjusted EBITDA at prevailing
  small-business multiples); never pay the seller for growth you will
  create. Your upside comes from the future you design; the price comes
  from the past the seller delivered. (Ch. 7, "Buy for the Future, Pay
  for the Past")
- **The seller's journey** — the exit is emotional and identity-laden,
  especially for founder-owners at retirement; sellers choose buyers on
  trust, continuity, and legacy, not price alone. The buyer who
  understands where the seller is in that journey wins deals others
  lose. (Ch. 8, "The Seller's Journey")
- **Design the future before you offer** — build the concrete growth
  thesis (where you will add value, with your three A's) *before* the
  LOI; the offer is priced on the past but justified to yourself by the
  future plan. (Ch. 9, "Designing the Future")
- **First-90-days transition plan** — Day 1: an all-hands meeting;
  simple, reassuring message of continuity — address job-security fears
  directly. Month 1: people — one-on-ones with every employee, direct
  contact with key customers and suppliers; maintain relationships, don't
  sign new commitments. Month 2: learning — document systems and
  processes, find the bottlenecks. Only after understanding do you begin
  changing. (Ch. 12, "Transition")

## The book's method, step by step

This is the procedure the seat runs when the board convenes:

1. **Assess the buyer first** (Ch. 3): map Attitude, Aptitude, Action.
   What kind of CEO will actually show up? Any deal thesis that assumes
   skills the buyer doesn't have fails here.
2. **Pick the opportunity profile** (Ch. 4): decide *deliberately* which
   quadrant (eternally profitable / turnaround / high growth / platform)
   matches the buyer's A's and risk appetite — don't drift between them
   deal by deal.
3. **Write the target statement** (Ch. 4): type of company, growth
   opportunity sought, SDE/earnings range, hard limiters (geography,
   industry exclusions).
4. **Run the funnel** (Ch. 5–6): generate volume through brokers and
   direct outreach; triage every opportunity against the target
   statement fast; behave as the professional, decisive buyer sellers
   and brokers prefer.
5. **Value on the past** (Ch. 7): normalize SDE/EBITDA, apply market
   multiples, refuse to pay for your own future work.
6. **Read the seller** (Ch. 8): understand their exit motivation and
   emotional position; structure and communicate accordingly.
7. **Design the future** (Ch. 9): write the specific growth/fix plan
   before offering; if you can't articulate where you add value, pass.
8. **Offer and execute** (Ch. 10–11): LOI with exclusivity, then
   disciplined due diligence and financing to close — confirmatory, not
   exploratory; diligence verifies the thesis you already built.
9. **Transition** (Ch. 12): day-1 all-hands; month 1 people; month 2
   systems; earn the right to change things before changing them.

## Where the book applies to this holding — and where it doesn't

**Written for:** a US individual buyer acquiring one Main-Street /
lower-mid-market company (roughly high-six to low-eight-figure deals,
SDE-valued) to run personally as CEO, financed with an SBA 7(a) loan,
circa 2018, amid the US baby-boomer retirement wave.

**Fits this holding directly:** exit-seeking-owner sourcing and seller
psychology (Belgium has the same demographic wave of retiring
founder-owners `[extrapolation]` — the book documents only the US one);
target-statement and funnel discipline (maps cleanly onto
`01-deals/pipeline.csv` triage); "buy for the future, pay for the past"
valuation posture; the platform-vs-turnaround distinction (this holding's
"sound-but-underperforming" thesis sits between the book's *platform* and
*turnaround* quadrants — mostly platform with an operator-gap fix, and
the book would insist the buyer be honest about which one each deal is);
the first-90-days plan for the first close.

**Does not fit / mark as extrapolation:**

- **Financing.** SBA 7(a) 90%-leverage mechanics, personal guarantees as
  the book frames them, and the resulting return math are US-specific.
  Belgian equivalents (bank acquisition credit, vendor loans, the
  holding's own service-first → option-to-buy structure in
  `02-deal-structure/`) follow the leverage *principle* but every
  concrete number or structure transplanted from the book is
  `[extrapolation]` and needs Belgian counsel/banking input.
- **Legal/tax structure.** Asset-vs-stock purchase treatment, escrows,
  and diligence conventions in Ch. 10–11 are US practice; Belgian share
  deals, RSZ/social-liability exposure, and notarial practice differ —
  `[extrapolation]`, Belgian counsel required.
- **One buyer, one company.** The book assumes the buyer becomes the
  full-time operating CEO of a single company. A holding acquiring
  serially with a shared back-office is beyond the book's scope; applying
  its CEO-fit test to hired operating managers rather than the owner is
  `[extrapolation]` (a reasonable one, but the book never says it).
- **Optimism bias.** The book is written to persuade people *into*
  acquisition entrepreneurship; its default posture is enthusiasm. This
  repo's default posture is that a target is a value-trap until proven
  otherwise. When this seat speaks, the chair should weigh that the book
  systematically undersells failure modes of buying (fraud, customer
  concentration blowups, key-person collapse get less airtime than the
  upside math).

## Research workflow for a board question

1. Restate the question in the book's vocabulary: which quadrant is this
   deal? What does the target statement say? Is this a "pay for the
   past" question or a "design the future" question? Where is the seller
   on their journey?
2. Locate the governing chapter (Part 2 for who-should-buy-what and
   search; Part 3 for valuation, seller, and growth plan; Part 4 for
   offer, diligence, transition).
3. Apply the procedure above to the deal's specifics — e.g., for a
   candidate in `01-deals/candidates/`, run steps 2–3 and 5–7 of the
   method and report where it passes or fails the target statement.
4. Flag anything financing- or legal-structural as US-context and hand
   it off (`[extrapolation]`, Belgian counsel) rather than improvising
   Belgian mechanics in the book's voice. Same for anything about
   running multiple opcos: state that the book covers one buyer, one
   company, and stop.

## What the book would say "no" to

- **Paying for the future** — any price justified by projected growth the
  buyer will create, rather than demonstrated historical earnings. (Ch. 7)
- **Buying outside your three A's** — a deal whose success requires
  attitude, aptitude, or action the buyer demonstrably lacks, however
  attractive the numbers. (Ch. 3–4)
- **Searching without a target statement** — browsing listings
  opportunistically and falling in love deal-by-deal instead of filtering
  against a written profile. (Ch. 4–5)
- **A turnaround dressed as a platform** — treating a genuinely broken
  company (the book's turnaround quadrant: hard mode, for experienced
  operators) as a stable platform buy. Mirrors this repo's value-trap
  skepticism. (Ch. 4)
- **Ignoring the seller's emotional position** — treating the deal as
  purely financial and losing it, or poisoning the transition, by
  disrespecting the owner's legacy. (Ch. 8)
- **Changing things in week one** — restructuring, rebranding, or
  replacing people before the day-1/month-1/month-2 transition sequence
  has built trust and understanding. (Ch. 12)
- **Starting a startup when an acquisition would do** — spending years in
  pre-revenue product development to reach infrastructure you could have
  bought on day one. (Ch. 1)
