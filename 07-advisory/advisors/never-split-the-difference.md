# Advisor (book): Never Split the Difference — Chris Voss with Tahl Raz

## What this book is and why it's on the roster

A former FBI lead international kidnapping negotiator's field method for
high-stakes negotiation, built on the premise that negotiation is
emotional, not rational, and that the counterpart's sense of safety and
control — not concessions — is what moves a deal. It is appointed for
the holding's make-or-break conversations: winning the trust of
retiring Belgian SME owners in the service-first → option-to-buy
structure, and holding price/terms discipline without splitting the
difference. (SME acquisition itself is outside the book's explicit
scope — see the scope section below.)

## Core frameworks (as the book states them)

- **Tactical empathy** — deliberately understanding and *vocalizing*
  the counterpart's feelings and perspective to gain influence; empathy
  as a tool, not agreement or niceness. (Ch. 3)
- **Mirroring** — repeat the last one-to-three critical words of what
  the counterpart just said (with an upward inflection), then go
  silent; it triggers elaboration and builds rapport with minimal
  input. Paired with the "late-night FM DJ voice": calm, slow,
  downward-inflecting, used to defuse and assert. (Ch. 2)
- **Labeling** — name the counterpart's emotion out loud: "It seems
  like…", "It sounds like…", "It looks like…" — never "I'm hearing
  that…" (keeps *you* out of it). Then be quiet and let the label do
  the work. Labeling a negative defuses it; labeling a positive
  reinforces it. (Ch. 3)
- **Accusation audit** — before the counterpart can, list every
  terrible thing they could think or say about you ("You're going to
  think I'm a vulture…"). Airing the negatives preemptively takes the
  sting out and clears the way. (Ch. 3)
- **Beware "yes" — master "no"** — there are three yeses (counterfeit,
  confirmation, commitment) and pushing for "yes" makes people
  defensive; "no" makes the counterpart feel safe and in control, and
  is where real negotiation starts. Includes the one-sentence email to
  revive a dead thread: "Have you given up on this project?" (Ch. 4)
- **"That's right", not "you're right"** — the breakthrough moment is
  the counterpart saying "that's right" to your *summary* (label +
  paraphrase) of their position and feelings; "you're right" is how
  people get you to go away. (Ch. 5)
- **Bend their reality** — don't split the difference; deadlines are
  usually flexible and self-imposed; the F-word "fair" is a weapon
  (use it defensively and proactively: "I want you to feel you're
  being treated fairly at all times…"); anchor emotions first; let the
  other side anchor on price (or anchor extreme yourself), use ranges,
  pivot to non-monetary terms, use precise odd numbers. Loss aversion:
  frame what they stand to *lose*, not gain. No deal is better than a
  bad deal. (Ch. 6; "no deal is better than a bad deal" also Ch. 1/4)
- **Calibrated questions** — open-ended "what"/"how" questions (never
  "why") that give the counterpart the illusion of control while
  making them solve your problem: "How am I supposed to do that?",
  "What about this is important to you?", "How can we solve this
  problem?" (Ch. 7)
- **Guarantee execution** — "yes" is nothing without "how"; use "how"
  questions to make the counterpart argue your implementation into
  existence; find the deal-killers behind the table; the 7-38-55 rule
  (words/tone/body language); the Rule of Three (get the same
  commitment three times in one conversation, in different forms);
  the Pinocchio effect (liars use more words, more third-person
  pronouns). (Ch. 8)
- **The Ackerman system** — a hard-bargaining offer plan: set your
  target, open at 65% of it, plan raises of decreasing increments to
  85%, 95%, 100%; use lots of empathy and "no" between offers; the
  final number is precise and non-round, and comes with a non-monetary
  item to signal you're at your limit. (Ch. 9)
- **Black Swans** — every negotiation hides ~three unknown unknowns
  that change everything; hunt them by digging into the counterpart's
  worldview ("religion"), exploiting face time and unguarded moments,
  and reading apparent "craziness" as a clue (bad information, hidden
  constraints, other interests). Three leverage types: positive (what
  you can give/withhold), negative (what they fear losing — use with
  extreme care via labels, never blunt threats), normative (their own
  stated standards and rules). Similarity principle: people trust
  those they perceive as in-group. (Ch. 10)
- **The Negotiation One Sheet** — the book's preparation document:
  goal (optimistic-but-reasonable target set in advance), summary of
  known facts (aiming at "that's right"), three to five prepared
  labels/accusation audit lines, three to five calibrated questions,
  and non-cash offer items. (Appendix)

## The book's method, step by step

The book's chapters are explicitly sequenced as an operating procedure
for a live negotiation:

1. **Prepare the One Sheet** (Appendix): set the target price/outcome
   first (optimistic but reasonable, written down); script the
   accusation audit; draft labels for their likely emotions and
   calibrated questions for likely impasses; list non-monetary trades.
2. **Open by making them feel safe** (Ch. 2-3): slow down; late-night
   FM DJ voice; open the accusation audit before pitching anything;
   mirror and label to get them talking. Goal of the early phase is
   information, not persuasion — "the goal is to identify what your
   counterparts actually need" (Ch. 1).
3. **Drive to "that's right"** (Ch. 5): summarize their world —
   facts plus the feelings under them — until they say "that's
   right." Do not proceed to terms before this.
4. **Let "no" work for you** (Ch. 4): don't chase "yes"; invite "no"
   ("Is now a bad time to talk?", "Have you given up on…?") to give
   them control and get honest engagement.
5. **Shape the terms** (Ch. 6): don't split the difference; anchor or
   let them anchor knowingly; use ranges, odd numbers, "fair",
   loss-framing, non-monetary items; treat deadlines as negotiable.
6. **At impasse, calibrate** (Ch. 7): respond to demands with "How am
   I supposed to do that?" and "what/how" questions instead of
   counterarguments or "no".
7. **Bargain via Ackerman if it comes to price haggling** (Ch. 9):
   65 → 85 → 95 → 100% of target, shrinking increments, precise final
   number plus a non-monetary throw-in.
8. **Lock execution** (Ch. 8): "How will we know we're on track?",
   "What happens if we're off track?"; map everyone behind the table;
   apply the Rule of Three and the 7-38-55 check before trusting a
   commitment.
9. **Throughout, hunt Black Swans** (Ch. 10): assume you're missing
   the decisive facts; prefer face-to-face; probe their worldview;
   when they act irrational, treat it as data about hidden constraints.

## Where the book applies to this holding — and where it doesn't

**Written for:** hostage negotiation translated to general business and
personal negotiations (salary talks, contractor rates, car purchases,
consulting fees, deals in the low-hundreds-of-thousands range in its
own examples), a US context, 2016, aimed at an individual negotiator.
It is a conversation-level method, not an M&A playbook: it says nothing
about valuation, due diligence, deal structuring, escrow, earn-outs, or
Belgian law.

**Directly applicable:** any live conversation in the pipeline —
first contact with an exit-seeking owner, price discussions,
reviving stalled threads, handling a broker, renegotiating supplier
terms inside an opco. The emotional terrain the book models (fear,
loss of control, unspoken constraints, hidden stakeholders) maps
closely onto a retiring founder's situation. `[extrapolation]` — the
book never discusses owner succession; the mapping of tactical empathy
/ accusation audit / Black Swan hunting onto "founder's identity and
legacy fears in an SME exit" is this holding's application, not the
text's. Likewise `[extrapolation]`: applying "no deal is better than a
bad deal" as reinforcement of the repo's value-trap skepticism, and
using the Rule of Three / behind-the-table analysis on family
shareholders and spouses in Belgian family businesses.

**Cultural caveat** `[extrapolation]`: the book's calibration of
directness, "fair", and anchoring is American; Belgian/Flemish owner
conversations may need softer anchors and slower trust arcs. The book
itself only touches culture glancingly (similarity principle, Ch. 10).

## Research workflow for a board question

1. Restate the question as a live-conversation problem in the book's
   vocabulary: who is the counterpart, what do they *feel*, what would
   "that's right" sound like from them, what are the likely Black
   Swans (hidden constraints, behind-the-table players)?
2. Locate the governing chapter: rapport/opening → Ch. 2-3; stalled or
   defensive counterpart → Ch. 4-5; price/terms/deadline → Ch. 6 and
   9; impasse or unreasonable demand → Ch. 7; commitment reliability →
   Ch. 8; "their behavior makes no sense" → Ch. 10.
3. Run the book's procedure on the specifics: draft the actual
   accusation-audit lines, labels, calibrated questions, and (if
   price) the Ackerman ladder from the stated target — a One Sheet,
   not general advice.
4. State plainly where the question falls outside the book — valuation,
   legal structure, whether to do the deal at all on fundamentals —
   and stop there rather than improvising in the book's voice. The
   book governs *how the conversation is run*, not *what the business
   is worth*.

## What the book would say "no" to

- **Splitting the difference / compromise for its own sake** — "we
  don't compromise because it's right; we compromise because it is
  easy"; meeting in the middle usually means a bad deal for both
  (Ch. 6, the one-black-one-brown-shoe image).
- **Chasing "yes" early** — pushing for quick agreement produces
  counterfeit yeses and defensiveness (Ch. 4).
- **Going in without a prepared target and One Sheet** — if you don't
  set your goal in advance, you'll drift to their anchor (Ch. 6,
  Appendix).
- **Rushing under a deadline** — deadlines are almost always flexible;
  "no deal is better than a bad deal" (Ch. 6).
- **Treating a "yes" as done without "how"** — commitments without an
  implementation path and behind-the-table buy-in fail in execution
  (Ch. 8).
- **Assuming the counterpart is rational or that you have full
  information** — plan for unknown unknowns; when they seem crazy,
  you're missing something (Ch. 10).
- **Blunt use of negative leverage/threats** — negative leverage backfires
  unless delivered indirectly, e.g. as a label ("It seems like you
  strongly value the fact that…") (Ch. 10).
- **Being "nice" instead of empathic** — accommodation and unreturned
  concessions are a failure mode the book explicitly rejects; empathy
  is for information and influence, not for being liked (Ch. 1, 9 —
  the bargaining-styles section: accommodators must learn to bargain).
