# Board session — First acquisition: plan of attack — 2026-07-09

## The question as framed by the chair

You asked for a *plan of attack for a first-time acquirer* — "I have no
acquisition experience, how do I go about it." Five seats answered that
question well and, in answering it, quietly proved it is not your real
question.

Every seat's advice reduces the "no experience" problem to *process*:
gates (HBR), a target statement (Buy Then Build), a written hurdle rate
(Leonard), a One Sheet (Voss), a circle of competence and an inversion
checklist (Munger). That is genuinely the right answer to inexperience —
and the fact that all five reach it by different routes is a strong
signal. But notice what none of the five actually validated: the one
thing your whole model rests on. Four of the five flag the
**service-first / option-to-buy structure** as `[extrapolation]` — outside
their documented record — and then endorse it anyway. The fifth (Voss)
speaks only to the conversation, not the structure. So the board's
apparent consensus on your best feature is not five confirmations; it is
five frameworks reasoning *by analogy* toward a structure none of them has
ever seen operate.

So the question the takes actually surface is not "how does an
inexperienced person run an acquisition?" It is:

> **Is the service-first / option-to-buy structure real — will Belgian
> exit-seeking owners and their accountants accept it — and can one person
> execute the search, the service delivery, and the holding admin it
> requires at the same time?**

Your binding constraint is not your ignorance of deals. It is *seller
acceptance* and *founder capacity*. Reframed that way, the first milestone
of your plan is not a deal, and not a better framework. It is evidence
about the structure itself. The rest of this synthesis is organized around
that reframe.

### Charlie Munger

**1. Position — the plan of attack**

You have no acquisition experience, so the plan is not "learn to acquire." It is: shrink the problem until your ignorance stops being fatal, then let the structure you've already designed buy you the education.

Concretely:

1. **Define your circle of competence in writing before you look at a single deal.** One or two sectors where you can actually judge whether the next ten years of a business's economics are foreseeable. Everything else goes in "too hard" — and almost everything should. (1994 USC speech: "in," "out," "too hard.") Your thesis says "no hard sector exclusion list yet" — I'd fix that. A man with no pattern library needs a narrow lane, not an open field. [extrapolation to your document]
2. **Run the service phase as a paid apprenticeship, deliberately.** Your service-first, option-to-buy structure is the best feature of this plan precisely *because* you're inexperienced: it buys information before it commits capital, and it limits irreversible downside. Do two or three service engagements with no intention of buying quickly. The big money is in the waiting. (Berkshire/Wesco meetings; structural application is [extrapolation].)
3. **Read five years of real accounts on every candidate, yourself.** Substitute "bullshit earnings" wherever EBITDA appears (Berkshire meetings, e.g. 2003). Normalize for the owner's undermarket salary and personal expenses run through the company [normalization specifics: extrapolation]. If you can't do this competently, learning it is your first job — before sourcing anything.
4. **Map incentives at every table.** Seller, broker, accountant, the manager staying on. Never trust a projection prepared by someone paid to make the deal happen ("The Psychology of Human Misjudgment," 1995). An exit-seeking owner is paid to have you believe the business runs without him.
5. **Pass fast and often.** Saying "no" in seconds is a competitive advantage. One good acquisition in three years is success; three mediocre ones is failure.

**2. Key reasoning**

Invert the question. Don't ask "how do I succeed with no experience?" — ask "how does an inexperienced solo buyer of small businesses destroy capital?" (1986 Harvard School speech method.) The answers: overpaying for a story, buying decline disguised as cheapness, trusting the seller's numbers, leverage, and deal momentum. Your plan of attack is simply the systematic avoidance of that list. Be consistently not stupid; brilliance is not required and, in your position, not available. Your inexperience is only fatal if you act at the speed of an experienced buyer.

**3. What I'd warn against most**

**Turnarounds seldom turn** (Buffett's 1979 letter, a position I endorsed for fifty years). You've branded the thesis "underperforming," which is a word that flatters value traps. The only underperformance worth buying is a sound business with a lazy or absent owner — your operator-gap verdict. Demand decay at any price is a cigar butt, and the cheap price is the trap, not the margin of safety (USC speech). And watch commitment-and-consistency: after six months of service work inside a company, the sunk effort will scream "buy it." That is a bias, not a signal. Even I got charmed by a story with Alibaba, late in life, with my own checklist in hand.

**4. What would change my mind**

Show me the first service engagement's *actual* figures after a year — real owner earnings, customers who stayed when the owner stepped back, a seller who told you the truth about something that hurt him. Demonstrated records over narratives, always. If the apprenticeship produces evidence instead of stories, I'd loosen the sector leash. Not before.

One caveat: Belgian SME law and micro-cap dynamics are outside my documented record. On those specifics — I have nothing to add.

### HBR Guide to Buying a Small Business (book)

**1) Position — the plan of attack, in the book's sequence**

The book's answer to "I have no acquisition experience" is: you don't need experience, you need its process, run as sequential gates that kill prospects cheaply before each more expensive stage.

1. **Decide honestly whether to do this at all** (ch. 1–3). Do you want to manage people, tolerate dull industries, sell yourself to sellers, and carry personal financial risk through a multi-year search? If not, stop.
2. **Fund and scope the search** (ch. 4–6). Treat the search as a full-time job of ~6–24 months plus ~3+ months to close. Budget living costs and search expenses up front; choose self-funded vs. sponsored. Write the target profile *before* looking — size band, recurring-revenue character, geography, acceptable industries — so filtering is mechanical, not mood-driven.
3. **Build a two-channel pipeline** (ch. 7–9). Brokered deals (volume, committed sellers, competition) and direct outreach to owners in chosen niches (proprietary, but many ambivalent sellers), in parallel. Expect hundreds of prospects per closed deal; kill most in minutes.
4. **Apply the dual filter** (ch. 10–12). (a) Enduring profitability — recurring customers, stable multi-year margins, low concentration, profits that survive the owner's exit. (b) A genuinely committed seller — reason for selling, price expectation stated early, life-after-sale plan. Fail either → drop without regret.
5. **Preliminary due diligence** (ch. 13). Your own time, days not months: rebuild owner earnings from raw numbers, normalize owner salary/perks, hunt deal-killers (concentration, owner-held relationships, deferred capex, litigation).
6. **Price and offer** (ch. 14–16). 3–5× normalized *historical* EBITDA, never seller projections; sketch a fundable stack (~40% senior debt, 20–25% seller note, ~35% equity); IOI, then LOI with exclusivity.
7. **Confirmatory diligence + financing in parallel** (ch. 17–19). Only after a signed LOI: accountants (quality of earnings), lawyers, and the money-raise together.
8. **Close, then don't break it** (ch. 20–21, Conclusion). Communicate continuity; learn before changing; manage cash weekly.

For the inexperienced founder specifically: the process *is* the substitute for experience — steps 2–4 are where first-timers fail, by searching part-time, skipping the written profile, and courting ambivalent sellers.

**2) Key reasoning**

Small firms trade at 3–5× for structural reasons (illiquidity, key-person risk, thin buyer pool); that discount — not a fix-it plan — is the buyer's return (ch. 14). Therefore buy proven, recurring, boring profits and pay for the demonstrated past. The gates exist because the searcher's scarcest asset is time.

**3) What the book warns against most — including about this thesis**

- **Turnarounds.** The book explicitly steers first-timers away from businesses that need fixing (ch. 10–11). "Sound but underperforming" is only acceptable to this seat if the recurring-customer base is provably intact *now* and the underperformance sits in the owner — the operator-gap verdict is compatible; anything shading toward demand decay is exactly what it forbids.
- **Ambivalent sellers** (ch. 12). The service-first structure risks institutionalizing them: an owner content to buy services indefinitely may never be a seller. The book would demand the dual filter's commitment test *before* the engagement, not after. [extrapolation — the structure itself is outside the book.]
- **Part-time searching** (ch. 4), falling in love with one prospect, spending confirmatory-diligence money before exclusivity (ch. 13, 16), paying for projections (ch. 14), and a seller who won't hold a note (ch. 15).
- Sub-€750k-EBITDA targets are below the book's band and, in its own terms, riskier (more owner-dependent). [extrapolation] Belgian law/tax/financing mechanics: outside scope entirely — the logic travels, the mechanics don't. [extrapolation; Belgian counsel required.]

**4) What would change this seat's answer**

Evidence per candidate that recurring revenue and margins have been stable through the underperformance; a seller who names a price and an exit date; the option-to-buy converting on pre-agreed terms (functioning like seller skin-in-the-game); and the founder committing real, budgeted, near-full-time search hours. Absent those, the book's verdict is: you are running a value-trap funnel with extra steps.

### Buy Then Build (book)

**1. Position — the plan, in the book's sequence**

1. **Self-assess before anything else (Ch. 3).** Map your three A's. *Attitude*: growth mindset, comfort managing risk. *Aptitude*: your actual skills — the book stresses creativity and solving issues as they arise over precision. *Action*: what you will actually do as an owner. Write it down. "No prior acquisition knowledge" is not disqualifying — the book is written for exactly that buyer — but a thesis assuming skills you lack fails at this step.
2. **Pick one opportunity profile deliberately (Ch. 4).** Your "sound-but-underperforming" thesis sits between the book's **Platform** quadrant (sound company with value and growth potential — the sweet spot) and **Turnaround** (broken company, hard mode, for experienced operators). Commit to *platform-with-an-operator-gap fix* and be honest, deal by deal, about which quadrant each target really is. As a first-timer, refuse turnarounds.
3. **Write the target statement (Ch. 4).** One sentence: "I am looking for a [product/distribution/service] company with [growth opportunity], generating [SDE range], with [limiters]." Your thesis already contains the raw material (€1M–€10M revenue, profitable, exit-seeking owner, commutable, employees beyond the owner) — compress it into the sentence and score every deal against it.
4. **Run the search as a full-time funnel, not browsing (Ch. 5–6).** Broker relationships, fast professional responses, rapid triage against the target statement; accept that the vast majority of opportunities die before LOI. Being a prepared, decisive buyer is itself deal flow. Your `pipeline.csv` is the funnel — feed it volume.
5. **Value on the past (Ch. 7).** Normalize SDE/adjusted EBITDA, apply prevailing small-business multiples, and never pay the seller for the improvement *you* will create. That improvement is precisely your thesis — so this rule is your margin of safety.
6. **Read the seller (Ch. 8).** Retiring founder-owners choose buyers on trust, continuity, and legacy, not price alone. Your service-first entry is, in the book's terms, a trust-building move `[extrapolation]` — the book never describes that structure, but its seller psychology strongly favors it.
7. **Design the future before offering (Ch. 9), then LOI, confirmatory diligence, close (Ch. 10–11)** — diligence verifies a thesis you already wrote; if you can't articulate where you add value, pass.
8. **Transition (Ch. 12).** Day 1 all-hands with a continuity message; month 1 people (one-on-ones, key customers/suppliers); month 2 systems and bottlenecks. Change nothing until then.

**2. Key reasoning.** The book's core claim (Ch. 1–2): buying profitable infrastructure beats starting from zero, and inexperience is compensated by *sequence discipline* — buyer fit first, target definition second, volume third, price discipline fourth. Knowledge is built in the funnel, not before it.

**3. What the book warns against most.** Searching without a target statement — falling in love deal-by-deal. Paying for the future. A turnaround dressed as a platform (the exact failure mode your "underperforming" thesis flirts with). Ignoring the seller's emotional position. Changing things in week one.

**4. Scope limits and what would change this answer.** The book assumes one US buyer becoming full-time CEO of one company on SBA 7(a) ~90% leverage. Belgian financing/legal mechanics, the multi-opco holding, hiring operators instead of operating yourself, and the cash+equity/option-to-buy structure are all `[extrapolation]` — Belgian counsel and banking input required; the leverage *principle* travels, nothing else does. This seat's answer changes if the 3-A's assessment shows you won't personally operate opco #1 — then the CEO-fit test transfers to a hired manager, which the book never addresses — or if early funnel data shows Belgian exit-seekers won't accept anything but full cash exits, which guts the entry structure. Note the book's known optimism bias; weigh this seat against the repo's value-trap default.

### Never Split the Difference (book)

**1) Position — the conversation plan of attack**

Your inexperience is not a weakness to hide; it is the first thing to put on the table, deliberately, before the owner does. The plan:

1. **Prepare a One Sheet before every owner contact** (Appendix): a written target for the engagement (not price yet — the goal of early meetings is information, Ch. 1), 3–5 accusation-audit lines, 3–5 labels, 3–5 calibrated questions, and non-cash items (the service phase itself, their advisory role, staff continuity).
2. **Open with the accusation audit** (Ch. 3): "You're probably going to think I'm young, that I've never bought a company, that I'm one of those people who wants your life's work for nothing, and that this 'service-first' idea is a trick to get inside your business." Say it all. It disarms precisely because it's true.
3. **Then say nothing about your offer.** Mirror and label your way into their world (Ch. 2–3, late-night FM DJ voice): "It sounds like selling was never the plan." "It seems like you're worried about what happens to your people." [extrapolation: succession/legacy fears are this holding's mapping — the book never discusses owner exits, but fear of loss and loss of control are exactly its terrain.]
4. **Drive to "that's right"** (Ch. 5): summarize their facts *and* feelings — the checked-out years, the absent successor, the identity tied to the firm — until they say "that's right." Do not discuss structure or price before this moment. This is the trust mechanism a no-track-record buyer needs; the book's answer to "I have no credibility" is: credibility comes from them feeling deeply understood, not from your CV.
5. **Use "no"-oriented openers** (Ch. 4): "Would it be ridiculous to keep running things exactly as you do now while we handle the back office?" "Have you given up on finding a way out of the business?" — the one-sentence email for stalled owners.
6. **When price talk starts** (Ch. 6, 9): let them anchor if you must hear their number, but knowingly; deploy "fair" proactively; frame in loss terms (what the business loses each year without a plan); if it becomes haggling, run Ackerman — 65/85/95/100% of a pre-set target, shrinking increments, precise non-round final number plus a non-monetary sweetener (e.g. their board seat, name on the building). [extrapolation: Belgian owners may need softer anchors and a slower arc than the book's American calibration.]
7. **Treat the service phase as Black Swan season** (Ch. 10): months inside the business is exactly the face time and unguarded moments the book says reveal the ~3 unknown unknowns — the spouse behind the table, the tax constraint, the key employee who'd walk. Apply the Rule of Three and 7-38-55 (Ch. 8) before believing any commitment to the option.

**2) Key reasoning.** Negotiation is emotional, not rational; the counterpart's sense of safety and control moves the deal (Ch. 1, 3). The exit-seeking owner's problem is fear, not spreadsheet logic — so the first-timer's disadvantage (no track record) matters less than the book's advantage (making them feel heard). The service-first structure *complements* the method: it operationalizes "yes is nothing without how" (Ch. 8) — commitment proven in execution before the big commitment. The tension: a service relationship tempts you into accommodation — being nice, absorbing scope, deferring the hard price conversation. The book explicitly rejects accommodator drift (Ch. 1, 9): empathy is for influence, not for being liked, and the option terms must be negotiated with full discipline despite the friendship built.

**3) What the book warns against most.** Splitting the difference on price or terms (Ch. 6); chasing an early "yes" from a polite owner (counterfeit yes, Ch. 4); entering any owner meeting without a One Sheet and pre-set target — you'll drift to their anchor; rushing because *you* feel deadline pressure to do a first deal ("no deal is better than a bad deal"); trusting a "yes" on the option without behind-the-table mapping of family and spouse [extrapolation to Belgian family firms].

**4) What would change this answer.** Outside the book's scope entirely — valuation, diligence, Belgian legal structure, whether the target is a value trap: this seat has nothing to say there. Within scope: if owners respond to the accusation audit with distrust rather than relief (a cultural calibration failure), soften and slow the arc; if the counterpart is a broker rather than the owner, the emotional map changes and Ch. 10 behind-the-table work becomes the opener, not the closer.

### Mark Leonard (modeled on public record — not consulted; may not represent his actual view)

**Caveat up front:** my documented record is vertical-market software — recurring, high-retention cash flows. Belgian non-software SMEs and the "underperforming" thesis are outside it; everything applied there is [extrapolation], and CSI itself only began building non-VMS competence in 2021 (2021 special letter). Take the method, not my multiples.

**1. Position — the plan of attack**

You don't have an acquisition problem; you have a dataset problem. CSI's underwriting edge is a proprietary base of hundreds of its own post-acquisition reviews (letters, passim). You have zero. So the plan is: build the machine that builds the dataset, and let the first deals be small enough that being wrong is tuition, not ruin.

- **Set a hurdle rate before you look at a single deal, and write it down.** CSI used ~20–30% IRR, higher for smaller/riskier deals, on probability-weighted scenarios (letters, esp. 2007–2015). With no base rates and a turnaround thesis, yours should sit at the top of that range or above. The hurdle is the whole discipline: if a deal doesn't clear it, the analysis ends. You never negotiate with your own hurdle — dropped hurdles are "magnetic" and rot everything you pursue afterward.
- **Go small, go repeated, go proprietary.** Small deals bought directly from founders face little competition; large deals mean auctions against financial buyers and inflated prices (letters on small-VMS strategy). Your €1–10M revenue band and owner-direct sourcing are correct. Never enter an auction.
- **Your service-first structure is, functionally, paid due diligence — use it that way.** [extrapolation] I have no documented analogue, but it maps to what I'd want: months inside the business observing real unit economics before the option is exercised. Underwrite the option at your hurdle *at signing*, not later when you're emotionally invested.
- **Write everything down.** I wrote letters for a decade analyzing Berkshire, Danaher, Roper before drawing conclusions, and reported my own weak organic growth honestly. Do post-mortems on every deal you pass on and every service engagement. After five or ten, you have the beginnings of base rates. That written record *is* your acquisition knowledge — there is no shortcut to it, only a compression of it.
- **Sell permanence.** Never-selling is a sourcing advantage, not sentiment: legacy-minded founders choose a permanent home over a PE flip (2014–2015 letters). "Buy to fix and keep" is your edge with exactly the exit-seeking Belgian owners you target — so never break it, even once.
- **Fund from cash flow, not leverage.** A perpetual owner cannot be a forced seller. Your structure already decouples entry from a war chest; keep it that way.

**2. Key reasoning**

Judgment is trained by many small, survivable, honestly-reviewed bets against a fixed standard. A first-time acquirer's real risk isn't ignorance — it's one oversized deal made on a narrative before any base rate exists.

**3. What I'd warn against most**

"Sound but underperforming." My hurdle logic buys quality of cash flows, not fixer-upper upside. With no data, you cannot yet distinguish an operator gap from demand-decay — a value trap looks identical from outside. Where no base rate exists, treat the projection as speculative: widen the downside scenario, or pass. And do not lower the hurdle to deploy faster; I sat on cash for years rather than stretch.

**4. What would change this seat's answer**

Real data. Three-plus completed service engagements with honest reviews showing your diagnosis calls were right would let you tighten hurdles and size up. Evidence you're being pulled into auctions or full-cash exits would mean the sourcing edge is gone — rethink the thesis. And on operating/growing the companies post-close: organic growth was my self-admitted weak spot — hear another seat.

## Contrarian seat

The five positions disagree on tactics and agree on architecture, and the architecture is where the trouble is.

**The consensus:** the service-first, option-to-buy structure is the plan's redeeming feature — "paid apprenticeship" (Munger), "trust-building" (Buy Then Build), "Black Swan season" (Voss), "paid due diligence" (Leonard) — and process discipline (gates, target statements, hurdle rates, One Sheets) substitutes for the founder's missing experience. Go slow, pass often, let the structure teach you.

**The case against it:** the structure every seat praises has never been observed working, by anyone, including the seats. Four of five explicitly flag it as `[extrapolation]` — then endorse it anyway. Notice what it actually requires: a solo founder with no acquisition record must first build and sell a *second, also-unvalidated business* — a fractional back-office consultancy credible enough that retiring Belgian owners pay for it partly in equity of their life's work. HBR demands a full-time search; Munger demands the founder personally master five years of accounts per candidate; Leonard demands a written post-mortem dataset; Voss demands prepared, patient owner courtship. Nobody adds up the hours. Three concurrent full-time jobs — search, service delivery, holding admin — for one person is not a plan, it's a queue.

**The shared unexamined assumption:** that the buyer's inexperience is the binding constraint and process is its antidote. It isn't, and it isn't. The binding constraint is *seller acceptance*: the thesis itself admits the structure filters out owners who want a clean cash exit — which is most exit-seeking owners. The residue who accept a slow, equity-paid entanglement with an unproven solo buyer are disproportionately HBR's ambivalent sellers and Leonard's adverse selection: businesses no conventional buyer would fund. The structure doesn't just source deals; it *selects for the worst ones*. Second shared assumption: that thoroughness is progress. Every seat's advice is more preparation — checklists, hurdle rates, sector definitions. For a founder who has never sold anything to an owner, the repo is already the failure mode: process artifacts accumulating in place of contact with reality.

**Failure in practice, years 1–3:** Year 1 — polishing frameworks, cold outreach converts poorly because the pitch ("pay me in equity to run your admin, and grant me an option on your company") is strange to Belgian sellers and their accountants; zero engagements or one, underpriced. Year 2 — founder is an overworked, underpaid back-office contractor to one or two marginal SMEs, holding illiquid equity slivers, search effectively part-time, no salary. Year 3 — the top-of-range hurdle plus pass-fast discipline means nothing ever clears, until sunk relationship cost does what Munger predicted: the founder exercises the option on the engagement they know, at a price justified by the relationship, not the numbers. The board's advice, followed faithfully, produces either paralysis or exactly one biased acquisition.

**What survives the attack:** pay only for demonstrated past earnings; operator-gap-only, no turnarounds; small, unlevered, direct-from-founder; test seller commitment early. Those hold regardless of structure. The falsifiable fix the board should demand: before any more process, ten real owner conversations in 90 days. If two won't discuss the structure seriously, the thesis — not the founder's preparation — is the problem.

### The short version

The board agrees on the buying rules but splits on your business model.
All five advisors back the same core: only pay for what a business has
already proven it earns, only buy businesses that are healthy but
under-managed, and write your rules down before you start looking. But
the board could not agree on whether your "work for the owner first, buy
later" structure is your greatest strength or a trap that attracts
exactly the wrong sellers — and nobody knows, because it's never been
tried in Belgium. So the recommendation is: stop preparing, and go test
it. Talk to ten real owners in the next 90 days. Their reaction — not
more planning — answers the question everything else depends on.

### What everyone agrees on

Five very different thinkers — a value investor, two how-to-buy
playbooks, a negotiation manual, and a man who bought hundreds of small
companies — independently landed on the same five rules:

1. **Pay for the past, not the future.** Value the business on what it
   has *already* been earning for years, checked by you personally —
   never on what the owner promises it could earn, and never on what
   *you* think you'll improve. Example: if a cleaning company made
   €200k/year for the last five years, that's what you price. The
   improvements you'll make are your profit, not the seller's sale price.
2. **Only buy "healthy but neglected."** The business must be sound
   today — loyal customers still buying, money still coming in — just
   tired because the owner has mentally checked out. Walk away from
   anything that needs *saving*. A business that's losing customers
   because the world moved on can't be fixed by good management, and
   "underperforming" is often just a kinder word for "dying."
3. **Small, no debt mountain, straight from the owner.** Buy at a size
   you can afford without dangerous borrowing, and find owners directly
   rather than bidding against professional buyers. Someone who plans to
   keep a company forever should never be forced to sell it because of
   one bad year of loan payments.
4. **Write your rules before you shop.** Your minimum return, your
   sectors, your walk-away conditions — on paper, *before* the first
   deal, because once you like a company you will be tempted to bend
   every rule for it. That's not a character flaw, it's how all human
   minds work; the written rule is the protection.
5. **Test the seller early.** Within the first conversations, ask for
   something concrete — real numbers, a meeting with their accountant. A
   serious seller gives it. An ambivalent one stalls, and every month
   you spend on a stalling owner is a month of your life gone.

Start following all five today. Nothing below changes them.

### Where the board disagrees

Most of the board says your service-first plan is your best asset: work
inside the company for a year as a paid back-office provider, build
trust with an owner who would never sell to a stranger, see the real
numbers from inside, and hold the right to buy at a pre-agreed price.
Free education, income, and a head start no other buyer has.

But one voice — the board's built-in devil's advocate — warns the
opposite: **the owners who accept this odd arrangement may be exactly the
ones you shouldn't buy from.** Think about it from the seller's side:
most retiring owners want a clean sale — money in the bank, done. An
owner who instead accepts a slow, complicated deal with an unproven
30-something who wants to be paid partly in shares... may be the owner
whose business no normal buyer would touch. The structure could quietly
filter *out* the good businesses and filter *in* the desperate ones.

The devil's advocate also made one point nobody could answer: **nobody
added up your hours.** Searching for companies is a full-time job (the
playbooks say so themselves). Actually delivering the back-office service
is another. Running the holding is a third. That's three jobs, one
person, for possibly years, on little or no salary.

Who's right? Genuinely unknown — the honest advisors admitted their
approval of the structure is educated guessing, since none of them ever
saw it tried. The good news: it's cheaply testable, which leads to:

### What to actually do

**This week:**
1. Adopt the five agreed rules above as written policy — put them in the
   investment thesis.
2. Do the money-and-hours audit: how many months can you live on little
   income, and who does the service-delivery work when you're in
   searching mode? Write the honest answer down.

**The next 90 days — the real assignment:**
3. Have real conversations with **ten** Belgian owners who are heading
   toward exit. Not to buy — to *test*. Present the idea honestly: "I
   run your back office, you pay me partly in a small stake, and I get
   the right to buy the company later at a price we agree now."
4. Use the negotiation book's approach: name their doubts *before they
   do* ("you're probably thinking — who is this young guy, why would I
   hand him shares..."), then listen. Aim for the moment they say
   "that's right," not a quick yes.
5. Score each conversation: did the owner (and their accountant) engage
   seriously, or did it land as strange? **If fewer than 2 of 10 engage
   seriously, the problem is the structure, not you** — come back to the
   board before spending a year polishing it.

**Decide-by (only you can, no advisor can help):**
6. Will you personally run the first company you buy, or hire a manager?
   All the first-timer playbooks assume you become the boss; your whole
   model says you'll employ operators instead. Decide which story is
   yours.
7. Is service-first your *only* way in, or one way among several? Keeping
   it exclusive is identity; loosening it is deal flow. Choose after the
   ten conversations, with data.
8. Pick your one or two sectors — the ones where you can actually judge
   whether a business is good. Your thesis currently says "no sector
   limits yet"; the value investor on this board would call that the
   first thing to fix.
9. Find one real, human Belgian M&A advisor or accountant. Every seat on
   this board is foreign or historical; none can tell you how a Belgian
   notaris, bank, or tax office treats your structure. That seat can't

## Chair synthesis

### Majority view

All five seats agree on a durable core, and — importantly — they reach it
from independent traditions, which is the strongest kind of agreement this
board produces:

- **Pay only for the demonstrated past.** Normalize owner earnings
  yourself; never pay for projections or for the improvement *you* will
  create (Munger, HBR 3–5× historical, Buy Then Build, Leonard's hurdle).
  This is the margin of safety, and it is grounded in every seat's
  documented record — high confidence.
- **Operator-gap only; refuse turnarounds.** "Underperforming" is a word
  that flatters value traps (Munger); the recurring-customer base must be
  *provably intact now* (HBR); a turnaround dressed as a platform is the
  exact failure mode your thesis flirts with (Buy Then Build); you cannot
  yet tell an operator gap from demand decay without data (Leonard).
  Grounded and unanimous.
- **Small, unlevered, direct-from-founder, no auctions** (Leonard, HBR,
  Buy Then Build). A perpetual owner cannot be a forced seller.
- **Write the discipline down before you look at a deal** — hurdle rate,
  circle of competence, target statement — and never negotiate with it.
- **Test seller commitment early, not after months of sunk service work**
  (HBR's dual filter, Munger's commitment-and-consistency warning, Voss's
  behind-the-table mapping, Leonard's "underwrite the option at signing").

Treat this core as adopted now. It does not depend on the structure being
right, and nothing below is a reason to defer it.

### Named dissents and their reasoning (preserved, not averaged)

The board does **not** actually agree about your central structure. The
disagreement is between the five seats' *endorsement-under-extrapolation*
and the contrarian's *attack*, and it decomposes into three distinct
conflicts. I name the type of each, because the type determines who can
resolve it.

**Dissent 1 — What is the binding constraint? (a conflict of PREDICTION,
not values.)**
The five seats assume the founder's inexperience is the thing to solve,
and that patient process plus a trust-building service phase overcomes it.
The contrarian says the binding constraint is *seller acceptance*: most
exit-seeking owners want a clean cash exit, so the residue who accept a
slow, equity-paid entanglement with an unproven solo buyer are
disproportionately the ambivalent and the unfundable — the structure
*selects for the worst deals*. This is not a values disagreement and no
amount of further analysis settles it. It is a testable empirical claim
about how real Belgian owners behave. Neither side has the data. Weight
accordingly: the board's confidence here rests on `[extrapolation]`, and
the contrarian's pessimism is equally an untested prediction.

**Dissent 2 — Does thoroughness equal progress? (a conflict of PREDICTION
about sequencing, with a temperament component.)**
The board's instinct is *prepare, then contact* — define the lane, build
the funnel, write the hurdle, then go. The contrarian's is *contact, then
prepare* — for a founder who has never sold anything to an owner, more
checklists are avoidance, and the repo filling with process artifacts is
already the failure mode. Note this is not a true contradiction: every
seat also wants early real-owner contact (HBR's commitment test, Munger's
"first engagement's actual figures," Leonard's post-mortem dataset, Voss's
entire method is contact). The genuine disagreement is only about
*ordering the first 90 days*, and it is resolvable by choosing to
front-load contact — which costs the board nothing it values.

**Dissent 3 — Is the structure a feature or a trap? (mixed: PREDICTION
plus a genuine VALUES call that is yours alone.)**
Munger, Buy Then Build, Voss, and Leonard read the service phase as the
plan's best feature — paid apprenticeship, trust-building, Black Swan
season, paid due diligence. The contrarian reads it as an
adverse-selection engine that also demands three concurrent full-time jobs
from one person. The predictive part (will owners accept it, does it
select badly) is testable. But underneath sits a values question the board
cannot decide for you: your operating model treats service-first as a
**hard filter and a core identity** ("buy to fix and keep," the agentic
back-office as the company's spine, Leonard's "sell permanence"). Keeping
it as a hard filter *is* the adverse-selection risk the contrarian names.
That trade — identity and trust-building versus deal-flow breadth — is a
choice, not a finding.

### Weighing the contrarian honestly

**What survives contact with the advisors' reasoning:**

- **The hours arithmetic survives, and it is the strongest point in the
  session.** Not one of the five seats costed the founder's time. HBR
  demands a near-full-time search; Munger demands the founder personally
  master five years of accounts per candidate; Leonard demands a written
  post-mortem dataset; Voss demands patient, prepared owner courtship; the
  service phase demands actual back-office *delivery*. That is three
  concurrent full-time roles for one person. The board did not rebut this
  because it never saw it. It stands.
- **The adverse-selection point survives — as amplification of something
  two seats independently conceded.** HBR warned the structure "risks
  institutionalizing ambivalent sellers"; Leonard warned you cannot yet
  distinguish an operator gap from a value trap. The contrarian is not
  contradicting the board here; it is naming the mechanism the board's own
  members flagged. That convergence *raises* the weight of the point.
- **The falsifiable test survives and is complementary, not hostile.** "Ten
  real owner conversations in 90 days; if two won't discuss the structure
  seriously, the thesis — not your preparation — is the problem" is exactly
  the early-commitment evidence HBR, Munger, and Leonard each asked for,
  operationalized into a gate.

**What does not survive intact:**

- **"The structure has never been observed working, therefore endorsing it
  is a failure of the board."** The seats did not hide the extrapolation —
  four of five labeled it explicitly. Endorsing the least-bad available
  entry for an inexperienced buyer with no war chest, while flagging it as
  unproven, is honest reasoning, not motivated reasoning. The correct
  response is to *test* the structure, which the contrarian itself
  demands — so this rhetorical charge collapses into agreement.
- **"A second, wholly unvalidated business."** Overstated. The agentic
  back-office is not a side venture bolted on; it is the holding's designed
  spine across the whole portfolio (operating model). The founder does not
  have to invent a consultancy from nothing. What *is* unvalidated is
  whether that layer is credible enough, early enough, that a retiring
  owner pays for it partly in equity — which is again a matter for the
  test, not a settled objection.

### The one actionable recommendation (disagreement left visible)

**Before building any more framework, run the contrarian's test as your
first 90 days — and instrument it to adjudicate the board's split with
evidence neither side currently has.**

Concretely: **ten real conversations with Belgian exit-seeking owners (and,
where you can, their accountants) in 90 days**, conducted with Voss's
method — accusation audit about exactly your weaknesses (young, no track
record, "pay me in equity to run your admin and grant me an option"),
labels and mirrors, drive to "that's right" before any price talk. The
*purpose* of these conversations is not to source a deal. It is to falsify
or confirm the structure. Record, per conversation: did the owner (and
accountant) engage the equity-paid, option-to-buy structure seriously, or
did it read as strange/untrustworthy? That single data series is what
separates the board's "best feature" from the contrarian's "adverse-
selection engine."

The disagreement stays visible in the gate:

- **The board's bet:** with patient, empathetic courtship, a workable
  fraction of owners will engage; the structure then delivers proprietary,
  trust-based, non-auction deal flow and free due diligence.
- **The contrarian's bet:** the serious-engagement rate will be near zero,
  and the few who bite will be the ambivalent and the unfundable.
- **The gate:** if roughly **fewer than 2 of 10** engage the structure
  seriously, the problem is the *thesis*, not your preparation — reconvene
  the board and reconsider the hard filter *before* spending a year on
  frameworks. If more do, the board's reading is provisionally supported and
  you proceed to build the funnel and hurdle in earnest.

Run in parallel, because the contrarian's hours point is unrebutted: **an
honest written time-and-money audit** — your monthly runway with little or
no salary for 18–24 months, and whether the service *delivery* work can be
scoped or staffed rather than consuming the founder whole. Adopt the
surviving core (pay for the past, operator-gap only, small/unlevered/
direct, written hurdle) now, unconditionally.

### What would change each side's mind

- **The board's mind changes if:** the ten conversations show owners and
  accountants won't engage the structure (rejection / adverse selection
  confirmed), *or* the honest hours audit shows the three-full-time-jobs
  problem is real and unstaffable at your runway — in which case the

- **The contrarian's mind changes if:** a meaningful fraction engage
  seriously *and* a credible path exists to staff or scope the service
  delivery so it doesn't consume the founder — in which case the structure
  sources exactly the trust-based, proprietary, non-auction flow the board
  claims.
- **Munger's specific unlock:** the first service engagement's *actual*
  figures after a year — real owner earnings, customers who stayed when the
  owner stepped back, a seller who told a truth that hurt him. Records over
  narratives; only then does he loosen the sector leash.
